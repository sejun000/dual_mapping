\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The flash translation layer (FTL) is the SSD firmware layer that maps every
logical page to its physical location. Because NAND flash cannot be overwritten
without erasing a whole block, physical pages are programmed sequentially within
a block and every update is written out of place, so the FTL maintains a mapping
table that translates each logical page to its current physical page. Most
commercial SSDs deployed today map at 4\,KiB granularity and keep this table in
on-device DRAM.

With QLC NAND, SSDs of 64 and 128\,TiB are being released and
developed~\cite{TODO-qlc-capacity}. At 4\,KiB granularity, a 64\,TiB SSD has
$2^{34}$ logical pages, so each entry needs at least 34 bits and the table takes
68\,GiB. A 128\,TiB SSD needs 35-bit entries and 140\,GiB. Providing this much
on-device DRAM is impractical because of its cost, power, and board
area~\cite{TODO-dram-cost}, so large SSDs have to translate addresses with a
mapping DRAM budget that is only a fraction of the full page-level table.

Techniques that reduce the mapping DRAM fall into two broad classes, and both
pay for it in latency.
\emph{On-demand mapping} (DFTL~\cite{dftl}, LeaFTL~\cite{leaftl}) keeps the
page-level table in flash and keeps part of it in an on-device map cache.
A read that misses in the map cache must first read its mapping entry from
NAND before it can read the data. This extra read is synchronous, so read
latency spikes whenever the map cache misses.
\emph{Block-level hybrid mapping} (BAST~\cite{bast}, FAST~\cite{fast},
Janus-FTL~\cite{janusftl}, CFTL~\cite{cftl}), on the other hand, maps most data
per NAND block and appends updates to page-mapped log blocks. When free space
runs low, the log blocks are merged back into their data blocks, and a merge
must copy all valid pages of a whole NAND block. Each 3D NAND generation
enlarges the NAND block~\cite{TODO-block-size}, so a merge copies more pages
and stalls host writes for longer. In our measurements, the p99 write latency
of FAST is 2.8--4.7$\times$ that of a page-level FTL under random I/O.

To avoid these latency problems, many commercial high-capacity SSDs use a
\emph{coarse indirection unit} (coarse IU) of 16--64\,KiB instead~\cite{TODO-iu}: the
whole coarse table fits in on-device DRAM, with no mapping misses and no block merges.
The cost moves to small writes. When a write is smaller than an IU, the FTL
reads the rest of the IU, merges the new data, and writes the whole IU again
(read-modify-write, RMW). This raises the write amplification factor (WAF) and
lengthens write latency.

We present \textbf{DUET} (\textbf{DU}al-granularity \textbf{E}lastic
\textbf{T}ranslation), an FTL that keeps the coarse IU but removes its RMW
by mapping data at two granularities under a fixed DRAM budget. Like hybrid
mapping it has a coarse and a fine level, but its coarse unit is the 64\,KiB IU,
decoupled from the NAND block. DUET has two key elements:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dual-granularity mapping.} Most data are mapped per 64\,KiB IU,
  which keeps the coarse table at 1/16 of a page-level table. Writes smaller
  than an IU are written out of place as 4\,KiB fragments, which a compact cuckoo
  hash index in DRAM tracks, so there is no RMW on the write path. Almost all of
  the mapping stays in on-device DRAM, so most reads need no mapping read from
  NAND and DUET avoids the read latency of on-demand mapping.
  \item \textbf{IU-level merging.} When the flash area for 4\,KiB fragments runs
  out of space, DUET reclaims it by merging the fragments of an IU into the IU
  and rewriting the IU, or by moving the fragments if that is cheaper. A merge
  rewrites one IU (16 pages) regardless of the NAND block size, and space is
  reclaimed by page-level garbage collection, so DUET never performs the
  block-sized merges of hybrid mapping.
\end{itemize}

We implement DUET and six other FTLs in NVMeVirt~\cite{nvmevirt}, a kernel-level
NVMe SSD emulator, with the same NAND timing, write buffer, and garbage collection
for all of them. We evaluate them with a 1/8 mapping-DRAM budget on real traces
(Alibaba cloud block storage~\cite{alibaba-trace} and SPFresh~\cite{spfresh}), key-value and OLTP
applications (YCSB~\cite{ycsb} on PostgreSQL and TPC-C), and raw random I/O.
DUET reaches 92\% of the throughput of a page-level FTL with unlimited DRAM (geometric mean),
compared to 79\% for LeaFTL and 68\% for DFTL. On the Alibaba trace it is
18\% faster than the page-level FTL. On the read side, its average read latency is up to 5.5$\times$ and
2.5$\times$ lower than DFTL's and LeaFTL's, respectively. On the write side, its p99 write latency is
up to 4.1$\times$ lower than FAST's (1.2--4.1$\times$ on every workload with a meaningful write share).

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We show that existing FTLs fit a limited mapping DRAM budget only by
  paying in read latency (on-demand mapping), merge cost and tail latency
  (block-level hybrid mapping), or RMW (coarse IU).
  \item We design DUET, which combines dual-granularity mapping with IU-level
  merging.
  \item We implement DUET and six other FTLs in a common emulator. Under the
  same DRAM budget, DUET achieves the highest average throughput among the
  bounded-DRAM FTLs, within 8\% of a page-level FTL with unlimited DRAM.
\end{itemize}
